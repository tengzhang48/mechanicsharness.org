What does the tip of a suddenly loaded crack feel in a fluid-filled solid?

Fluid trapped inside a solid makes it harder to deform. After a sudden load, most of the solid around a crack still holds its fluid, but fluid can escape from a small region around the tip. You will find the crack volume and pore pressure, then decide which response sets the stress intensity at the tip.

You will practise
  • choosing the right limit at each scale;
  • making two hand estimates of a difficult answer;
  • choosing a check that can actually fail.
You need
  • the elastic crack opening, stress intensity and energy release rate from the introductory page;
  • basic solid mechanics and integration of a crack opening;
  • pen and paper, and a calculator. The page supplies the numerical tip calculation.

1 The problem

A fluid-saturated solid has connected small spaces, called pores, filled with fluid. A Biot poroelastic solid is a model in which deformation of the solid framework and pressure of the fluid in those pores affect each other. An infinite body contains a stationary crack from x = −L to L on y = 0. At time t = 0, a pressure σ is suddenly applied to both faces and held there, giving a normal stress −σ. The faces are kept drained: the pore pressure on them is held at zero. The body is in plane strain: displacement out of the picture is zero, and the in-plane displacements do not vary in that direction.

Drained Poisson ratio, the elastic ratio of sideways contraction to extension when fluid can leaveν0.2
Undrained Poisson ratio, with fluid kept in placeνu0.4
Biot coefficient, measuring how strongly pore pressure acts on the solid frameworkα0.89
The remaining constants only set scalesG, L, σ and c

G is the shear modulus, the stiffness relating shear stress to shear strain. L is the half-length of the crack, and σ is the applied face pressure. c is the diffusivity, which sets how quickly pore pressure spreads. Their numerical values are not needed for the dimensionless answers.

Undrained means that each bit of material keeps its fluid during the response being considered. Drained means that fluid has time to move and the induced pore pressure can relax. The undrained solid is stiffer than the drained solid here. Applied pressure on a crack face and pore pressure in the surrounding solid are different quantities: the load remains σ even though the pore pressure on the face is zero. See drained and undrained response.

Diffusion is the spreading and relaxation of a pressure difference through fluid motion. In time t, that change reaches a distance of about √(ct). This is the diffusion length, not the location of a sharp boundary. See how far pore pressure diffuses.

Problem: a suddenly pressurized crack in a fluid-saturated porous solid

An infinite, homogeneous, isotropic, fluid-saturated porous solid deforms in plane strain (x-y plane). A crack occupies −L ≤ x ≤ L, y = 0. The solid obeys Biot's linear poroelasticity, with shear modulus G, drained Poisson's ratio ν, undrained Poisson's ratio ν_u (ν < ν_u ≤ ½), Biot coefficient α (0 < α ≤ 1) and permeability coefficient κ.

In terms of the total stress σ_ij (tension positive), the pore pressure p, the strain e_ij and the variation of fluid content per unit volume ζ:

  • 2G e_ij = σ_ij − ν σ_kk δ_ij + α(1 − 2ν) δ_ij p
  • 2G ζ = α(1 − 2ν) σ_kk + α²(1 − 2ν)² p / (ν_u − ν)
  • equilibrium: σ_ij,j = 0
  • Darcy: q_i = −κ p,i
  • mass balance: ∂ζ/∂t + q_i,i = 0

(indices 1, 2; summation implied). The diffusivity is c = 2κG(1 − ν)(ν_u − ν) / [α²(1 − 2ν)²(1 − ν_u)].

Stresses and pore pressure are zero everywhere for t < 0 and vanish at infinity. Two loadings act on both crack faces (−L ≤ x ≤ L) for t > 0. Here σ_n = σ_yy is the normal stress on the faces and H is the unit step.

  • Loading 1: σ_n = −H(t), p = 0.
  • Loading 2: σ_n = 0, p = H(t).

Let K be the mode I stress intensity factor at the crack tips, and V the crack volume per unit thickness (the integral of the opening over the crack; positive for an open crack).

(a) For loading 1, find K and V just after loading (t = 0⁺) and at long times (t → ∞). Find also the leading-order growth of V at small times, ct/L² ≪ 1.

(b) For loading 2, find K at t = 0⁺ and as t → ∞, and V as t → ∞.

(c) Evaluate your results for ν = 0.2, ν_u = 0.4 and α = 0.89.

Report in results.json (for (c); K in units of √(πL), V in units of L²/G, per unit loading magnitude):

  • K1_0, K1_inf, V1_0, V1_inf: loading 1 at t = 0⁺ and t → ∞;
  • V1_short_coeff: the coefficient A in V ≈ V1_0 + A √(ct/L²) for loading 1 at small times;
  • p_faces_0: in loading 1, the pore pressure induced in the solid immediately next to the crack faces at t = 0⁺, before it diffuses;

2 The physical picture

Compare the size of the crack with the distance fluid has had time to move. Move the time slider to grow the drained layer along the faces and around each tip. The shading marks the scale √(ct), not a sharp boundary between materials. Fluid very close to the tip has only a short distance to travel to a drained face. It can therefore drain at any positive time, even while the layer is too small to see at the scale of the whole crack.

  1. The load has both effects. The pressure pushes the faces apart and, at the same time, compresses the solid beside them. That compression raises the pore pressure.
  2. Fluid drains to the faces. Their pore pressure is held at p = 0. The distance over which drainage changes the response grows as √(ct).
  3. The tip is inside the drained layer at every positive time. At short times most of the crack still responds as undrained, while the material close enough to the tip already responds as drained.
  4. At long times, the induced pore pressure has relaxed. The response is drained throughout the region relevant to the crack, and the crack has opened further.

Step 3 Which stiffness does the tip feel?

Background: diffusion length, the elastic tip field, opening and intensity, energy release rate.

Fluid needs time to travel a distance. That distance is very different for the whole crack and for material close to its tip. The drainage time follows from the diffusion length:

td(l) is the time needed for pressure to diffuse over distance l, and c is the diffusivity. The symbol ∼ means that we compare scales and leave out constant factors. Over the crack length L, drainage takes a time of order L2/c. Over distance r from the tip, it takes a time of order r2/c. A time much shorter than L2/c can therefore still be long enough for drainage sufficiently close to the tip.

What happened

The tip contribution is of order (ct)1/2, and both agents pass Test 1. Agent B’s elastic checks pass because they inspect only the crack scale, where B is right. 6 Take away

A short time is short only at some scales

ct/L2 much smaller than 1 makes the crack-scale response undrained. But r/√(ct) tending to zero makes the tip response drained. Before choosing a material response, name the distance scale and state which limit comes first.

Small regions near a singularity are not negligible

The tip zones have area of order ct, but both the weighting stress and the draining pore pressure grow near the tip. Together they give a contribution O(t1/2), the same time dependence as the faces. Count every singular factor before discarding a small region.

A check that shares the assumption will pass

Whole-crack elastic checks inspect quantities controlled by the crack scale. They cannot tell whether the tip response was chosen correctly. To test that assumption, choose a calculation or limit that examines the tip region, rather than another whole-crack quantity.

7 Extra exercise: pore pressure on the faces

Now change the loading. The faces carry no traction, meaning no force per unit area, but the pore pressure on them is suddenly raised to p0 and held there. This is loading 2 of the task. At the first instant the pressure change has not spread into the solid, so there is no sudden opening:

K2 is the dimensional intensity for this second loading. Its initial value is zero because the faces are not mechanically loaded and fluid has not yet moved into the surrounding material.

At long times, the region reached by the pore pressure would like to swell. The unpressurized solid around it restrains that expansion, like a heated spot in a cold plate. This restraint compresses the pressurized region. The long-time solution gives the following uniform back-stress, or stress from restraint, which we use as a supplied result:

σback is the compressive back-stress and η is its dimensionless coefficient. This η is different from the tip coupling ηt. p0 is the imposed pore pressure, while α and ν are the Biot coefficient and drained Poisson ratio.

The back-stress is a stress inside the pressurized region around the crack. It is not a traction applied to the faces, which stay free of traction. Keeping them free in a region under this compression closes the crack. The final intensity and volume are those of a drained elastic crack under pressure −ηp0:
