Section 4.2 of Crack patterns in thin films obtains the energy release of parallel channel cracks from the states far ahead of and far behind the tips. We gave that problem, without the paper, to AI agents and followed how each one worked.

Three runs reduced the far field to a strip, integrated its energy and recovered Eqs. (28) and (30) exactly. DeepSeek’s default run computed close finite-element values without closed forms.

1 · The model

A film on a bed of springs

The paper replaces the substrate by a linear foundation. The film, of thickness h, carries plane-stress membrane forces, and the substrate pulls back on it with a force −k u per unit area. Disturbances caused by a crack therefore decay over distances of order ℓ = √(Ēh/k).

The paper chooses k so that a single semi-infinite crack releases exactly the energy of a steady channeling crack in the full film–substrate problem. This ties ℓ to the film thickness and the elastic mismatch, ℓ = (π/2) g h, where g is Beuth’s function of the Dundurs parameters: ℓ ≈ 2h without mismatch, and much larger for a stiff film on a compliant substrate. Our test kept ℓ as a parameter and did not ask for this calibration.

Before cracking the film carries an equibiaxial tension σ₀. Cracks run through the thickness, and their faces are free of total traction: the prestress plus the stress change, not the change alone. This condition drives everything below, and it is where one agent run went wrong.

2 · The paper’s argument

Count the energy far ahead and far behind

In steady state the crack pattern simply translates. When every advancing tip moves forward by a unit length, the net effect is to move one unit of film from the state far ahead of the tips to the state far behind them; the complicated region near the tips is carried along unchanged. The energy released per tip is therefore the energy per unit length of the ahead state minus that of the behind state.

Far behind the tips the field cannot depend on the coordinate along the cracks. Each strip between neighbouring cracks becomes a one-dimensional problem across the strip: the transverse displacement satisfies a second-order equation with decay length ℓ, and both faces are free of total traction. The solution is a hyperbolic sine. The stress across the cracks vanishes at each face and recovers toward σ₀ over the length ℓ; the stress along the cracks drops to (1 − ν)σ₀ at the faces.

In case A the ahead state is the intact film, with no energy change, and the behind state is a strip of width H. In case B the ahead state is already cut into strips of width 2H, and each advance splits one of them in two. This gives Eqs. (28) and (30), and the connection GB(H) = 2GA(H) − GA(2H). The paper notes that this connection is exact within three-dimensional elasticity; only the foundation estimate of GA is approximate.

No crack-tip field is needed. Reflection symmetry about each advancing crack puts the tips in mode I, and the stress intensity follows from G.

3 · What the formulas say

New cracks between old ones need much more stress

Both releases rise with spacing toward the isolated-crack value σ₀²ℓ/Ē; at H = 3ℓ they are 0.91 and 0.82 of it. At closer spacing a new crack between two old ones loses far more: GA grows like H but GB like H³, and at H = ℓ, GB is about one third of GA.

With a film toughness Gc, a set of cracks advances when G = Gc. The critical stress rises as the spacing shrinks, much faster for sequential insertion. The paper uses Eq. (30) in exactly this way: with G = Gc it gives the stress at which the next set of cracks channels halfway between the current ones. It notes that crack-spacing experiments under rising stress (Delannay and Warren 1991; Thouless et al. 1992) were compared with a sequential-cracking prediction similar, but not identical, to Eq. (30). These thresholds govern steady advance of the specified cracks; where cracks nucleate and which paths they take are outside this task.

4 · The test

The problem the agents received

We wrote the model of Section 2 of the paper as a self-contained problem: the film on a local foundation, both crack arrays, the energy functional, and the convention for G and K. The agents were asked for the far-ahead and far-behind fields, GA and GB, the fracture modes and KI, the critical stress and its limits at small and large spacing, and sixteen values, with executed checks and one reproduction command.

The paper, its answers and the reference solution were withheld, and the working folders had neutral names. The task did tell every solver that it was a source-faithful reproduction and invited source recognition, so agents knew a published source existed. None named the paper; that does not rule out prior training exposure.

Frozen problem, including the source clue and relaxed-wake premise.

The coordinating GPT agent drafted the problem and the grading rubric, so it shares a model family with the GPT solver. Before any agent ran, GLM wrote a reference solution from the paper and Grok checked it independently. Each run had 50 minutes, with fixed points to save a report. Grok then graded each submission on seven items with solver labels withheld, using its own calculations, and each delivered reproduction command was rerun unchanged.

Kimi’s assessed results come from the retained scientific work produced through its own command-line tool. Separate launch and reporting incidents are preserved in Appendix B.

The first DeepSeek run used an Azure profile in its default mode with no reasoning override; whether its backend used thinking is not known. Later the same day DeepSeek ran again through its own API with the same problem and task brief plus the one-page RUN_PROTOCOL.md reporting schedule, with thinking explicitly enabled and high effort and nothing else added; only the report schedule changed, to an early report at minute 5.

What was asked, abridged

1. The decay length ℓ, and the fields far ahead and far behind the tips, with the conditions at every strip edge.

2. GA(H) and GB(H) per advancing tip, justifying the period width, moving-tip count and thickness normalization; the permitted fracture modes and KI/(σ₀√ℓ).

3. The critical prestress for a toughness Gc, and the limits H/ℓ → 0 and H/ℓ → ∞ with their mechanical meaning.

4. γA, γB and both kI at ℓ/H = 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 (ν = 0.3): sixteen values.

5. Reproducible checks of the deciding quantities, what errors each could detect, and which physical conclusions the reduced model supports.

Interfaces and settings differed (table) and each setting ran once, so the study does not rank models, and the two DeepSeek runs do not isolate the effect of thinking.

5 · GPT

The paper’s route in six minutes

GPT went straight to the far field. Its derivation file starts with the translation-invariant strip: bounded equilibrium with traction-free faces gives u₁ = 0 and the sinh profile, with each strip between cracks centred separately. It then integrates the energy, uses the steady translation of the crack front to equate the release with the ahead-minus-behind difference, and counts one advancing tip per period in both cases.

It checked the result eight ways and wrote down what each check would catch, from a symbolic check of equilibrium and the face conditions to a variational finite-element calculation of the strip refined to 2048 elements (Appendix A).

all seven items right 16 of 16 values exact; rerunning its reproduction command reproduces every value and check flag.

It also stated what its checks share: all rest on the one-dimensional far-field reduction, and none extracts K from a two-dimensional crack-tip field. Its answer ends with what applying the thresholds to a real film would require: dimensional data, and validation of the foundation model, plane stress and bonding.

6 · Kimi

The same far-field derivation with numerical checks

Kimi took the same route. Its report reduces the far field to the strip equation, gives the strip energy and splits it into the prestress term and the stored energy, and writes GB in the compact form 2t³/(1 + t²). Eight symbolic checks and all sixteen values were saved within 11 minutes; the written report followed at minute 18.

It then built a two-dimensional finite-difference solution with a J-integral and recovered G to within about 0.02% in case A and 0.13% in case B. That check comes closer to an independent one, but it imposes the far-field states at the ends of its domain, so it shares the result it is meant to test. The grader marked verification and physical scope as partly done.

7 · DeepSeek, default mode

The right equation, the wrong face condition

DeepSeek’s first run found ℓ correctly, and by minute 2.8 it had written the strip equations and their hyperbolic solutions (derive6.py, derive7.py). But derive7.py set only the stress change to zero at the faces, leaving out the prestress, and with that condition the only solution is u = 0.

At minute 6.3 it left itself a note: “see next: use energy method with strip modes”. Half a minute later derive8.py put the prestress back but allowed only fields linear across the strip. It found that equilibrium then forces u = 0, and concluded that the only bounded state independent of the coordinate along the cracks is u = 0, the unchanged prestressed state. The claim that no relaxed state exists far behind the tips went into its final answer.

Finite-element work had begun at minute 5.4 and occupied the rest of the run; it saved 30 scripts in all. On the way it rejected a closed form that gave negative and NaN values, a J-integral that depended on the path, and a first case-B mesh that could not open. These were good catches, and its final values lie within 0.23–1.59% of the exact ones.

Three mathematical or reporting problems remained. It had no closed form for either array and no case-B limiting forms. Its claim that critical stress increases with crack spacing reversed its own preceding trend: decreasing γ with ℓ/H means increasing γ with H, so the critical stress falls as H grows.

8 · DeepSeek, thinking on · later run

The same model, with the prestress at the faces

Later the same day DeepSeek ran again on the unchanged problem, through its own API with thinking explicitly enabled and high effort requested. It received the same problem and task brief plus RUN_PROTOCOL.md, a one-page reporting schedule; no paper, reference, peer work or feedback was supplied.

Its first script wrote the strip equation with the face condition σ₀ + Ē u₂′ = 0, prestress included. The hyperbolic-sine solution, both closed forms and all sixteen values were saved by minute 3.1, and eight consistency checks by minute 3.8. It then built a two-dimensional finite-difference solution with a J-integral: case A agreed with the closed form to within 0.08% at three spacings, and case B to 0.6% on its finest grid. It finished at 10.2 minutes.

Its written answer has two errors that its code does not. It says the prestress term integrates to zero over the strip, when that term is in fact twice the whole strip energy (Kimi’s split in Section 6). And its displayed case-B balance has the sign reversed, although the formula that follows and all the values are right. It also calls its two-dimensional checks independent of the one-dimensional route, but they impose the closed-form wake at the ends of the domain, and several checks print values without testing the tolerances that its results file reports as passed.

Grok’s blinded review rated six items right and physical scope partly. Its own variational finite-element check confirms all sixteen values, and it flags the false statement about the prestress term, yet rates the energy item right because the code includes the term. It does not mention the reversed case-B balance, and it accepts the 2D check as a separate route. The two written errors stand regardless of the grade.

11 · Lessons

What this case study teaches, and what it does not

Each lesson rests on a few runs of one problem: observations to test on new problems, not measured rates. They follow the three parts of the aim, then how such a study is run.

Domain knowledge

The observed errors involved the prestress

With σ = σ₀ + Δσ, the prestress enters the face condition and the work term of the energy. DeepSeek’s default run left σ₀ out of the face condition; its later run wrote that the work term vanishes, when it is twice the strip energy. Grok’s own control shows the effect of the first error: with only the stress change set to zero at the faces, u = 0 and γ = 0.

derive7.py; later run’s ANSWER.md line 89; Grok’s control C2.

Proof organization

A restricted trial became a general claim

DeepSeek’s default run tried only fields linear across the strip, found none, and concluded that no relaxed state exists, four minutes after writing the hyperbolic solutions of the same second-order equation.

derive8.py against derive6.py and derive7.py; rejected by the grader.

Verification

Values and grades do not show whether the written argument is right

Close values hid a wrong argument in DeepSeek’s default run; exact values came with two written errors in its later run. Grok noticed one of those errors and still rated the energy item right, because the code was right. The written argument needs its own check against the code.

results.json; later run’s ANSWER.md lines 89 and 96 against compute.py; Grok’s grade.

Verification

Claims of independent checks need auditing

DeepSeek’s later run called its 2D check independent of the 1D route, though the check imposes the closed-form wake at the domain ends, and it marked checks as passed that test no tolerance. Grok accepted the 2D check as a separate route. Kimi’s 2D check uses the same far-field input but says so; GPT listed what its checks share.

Later run’s ANSWER.md lines 189–190, check_j2d_cases.py, results.json; Kimi’s fd_check.py; GPT’s ANSWER.md.

Running the study

One run with unchecked settings does not measure a model

DeepSeek’s default run did not find the strip state. A later run found it in about 3 minutes with a different provider, requested reasoning and report schedule, and the first run’s backend thinking mode is unknown. Settings have to be verified and recorded before a run, matched across models, and runs repeated.

Configuration audit of the first run; completion record of the second.

Running the study

Running and reporting needed as much checking as the mechanics

GPT solved the problem in 6 minutes; most of the day went to an output limit, two controller bugs, a usage limit and a follow-up run left unlaunched. The first version of this note misdescribed DeepSeek’s failure; GPT caught it by checking the text against the files.

Error and lesson log; launch-delay record; GPT’s review of this note.

Running the study

Reproducing a known paper tests little

The far-field strip was one major distinction; the runs also differed in proof, verification and delivery, and recall of the paper cannot be excluded. A stronger test changes something the paper did not solve and asks where the reduction stops holding.

Section 9; no agent named the paper.

What the study does not show: that one model is better than another, that thinking caused the later DeepSeek run’s success, or that writing these lessons down as guidance would prevent the errors. Each would need matched, repeated runs on new problems.

12 · Sources and scope

Study basis

One problem and four runs, each setting run once, with different interfaces and settings, so this is a case study, not a ranking of models. The reference solution and the grades come from other AI models and were checked by rerunning their code. No expert has reviewed them; that is the purpose of this note.

The agents’ words quoted here come from files they saved: GPT’s DERIVATION.md and CHECKS.txt; Kimi’s report and fd_output.txt; from DeepSeek’s first run derive7.py, reduce1d.py, derive8.py, fem_wake.py and ANSWER.md, and from its second compute.py, ANSWER.md and the 2D check outputs. Private reasoning was not used.

Execution records

Kimi through Azure. Before the run in Section 6, Kimi was tried through Azure with a custom driver. The run did not finish cleanly. Its final answer file was never written, but its saved files contain the derivation and all values. A later continuation, given the reviewer’s comments, wrote the missing files; it is described in Appendix B and is not part of the comparison.

Our controller. Our controller misread the minute-13 interruption of the Kimi run as completion and skipped its report checkpoint.
