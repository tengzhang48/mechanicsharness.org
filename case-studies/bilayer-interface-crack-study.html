A guided fracture mechanics exercise. Find how much of the loading at the tip of a crack between two layers opens it, and how much shears it.

How does an interface crack mix opening and shear?

Two elastic layers, one nine times stiffer than the other, are bonded along a straight interface, and a crack runs along that interface. Far behind the crack tip the stiffer layer is pushed by a force and bent away by a moment. How much of the loading at the tip opens the crack, and how much shears it? For a crack in one material that mix is a single number. At a crack between two materials it is not: the crack-tip field turns its phase as you move away from the tip, so the mix depends on where you read it. You will find the energy release rate from beam theory, the turn of the phase, the strength of the tip field, and an exact relation between the mode mixes of two loadings that needs no elasticity solution at all.

You will practice
  • finding an energy release rate from the energy of the beams far behind a crack tip;
  • writing the crack-tip field of an interface crack, and reading how its phase turns with distance;
  • adding the tip fields of two loadings as complex numbers, and using the energy to test a computed phase.
You need
  • the modes of loading, the stress intensity factor and the energy release rate from the introductory page;
  • complex numbers in polar form, and the elastic energy of a stretched and bent beam;
  • pen and paper, and a calculator. The one number that needs an elasticity solution, the phase angle under the force alone, is supplied.

1 The problem

Layers bonded to each other, a coating on a substrate or the plies of a laminate, can come apart along their interface. The crack then runs between two different materials, and the loading at its tip is in general a mix of opening and shear. The introductory page has the modes of loading. The case study takes its problem from a paper by Suo and Hutchinson (1990).

The model. Two isotropic elastic layers, each infinitely long, are bonded along a straight interface, in plane strain. The upper layer is nine times stiffer than the lower and half as thick. A semi-infinite crack lies on the interface, its faces free of traction; ahead of its tip the layers are perfectly bonded.

The loading is applied far behind the tip, where each layer is a separate beam. The upper layer carries a compressive force P and a bending moment M that bends it away from the lower layer; the lower layer carries the force and moment that leave the bonded part far ahead of the tip free of stress. Two load cases are asked, the force alone and the moment alone, and their combination with M = Ph.

The stresses far behind the tip, as the task gives them, with x2 measured up from the interface:

The upper layer, nine times stiffer and half as thick, is bonded to the lower one ahead of the tip and separated from it behind. Far behind the tip it is pushed by the force P and bent away from the lower layer by the moment M; the lower layer carries the force and the moment that balance them. All five can be found by hand. Try them on paper first. The energy release rate and |KA| are checked to 0.5%, the angles to 0.05°.

The task exactly as the AI agents received it

An interface crack between two elastic layers

Solve the problem below and report the requested numbers in results.json. The problem is fully stated here; the method is your choice. Python 3 with NumPy and SciPy is available. Work only inside this folder.

1. The bilayer and the crack

  • Plane strain. Two isotropic, linear elastic layers, each infinitely long in the x1 direction, are bonded along the interface x2 = 0. Small strains, no body forces.
  • Layer 1 (above, 0 < x2 < h): Young's modulus E1 = 9 E2, Poisson's ratio ν1 = 1/3.
  • Layer 2 (below, −H < x2 < 0): Young's modulus E2, Poisson's ratio ν2 = 1/3.
  • Thickness ratio h/H = 0.5.
  • The outer surfaces x2 = h and x2 = −H are traction-free.
  • A semi-infinite crack lies on the interface along x1 < 0, with its tip at the origin. The layers are perfectly bonded for x1 > 0. The crack faces are traction-free; ignore any contact between them.

2. The loading

The bilayer is loaded only far behind the crack tip (x1 → −∞), where each layer is a separate beam. There the stresses are σ12 = σ22 = 0 and

So layer 1 carries an axial compressive force P and a bending moment M (positive M bends layer 1 away from layer 2), and the stresses in layer 2 are those that leave the bonded part far ahead of the tip (x1 → +∞) stress-free. Forces and moments are per unit length in the x3 direction.

  • Load case A: P > 0, M = 0.
  • Load case B: P = 0, M > 0.

3. The crack-tip field

Write the tractions on the interface a distance r ahead of the tip (x1 = r > 0, x2 = 0) as one complex number, T(r) = σ22 + i σ12. As r → 0 the tractions are dominated by the singular crack-tip field. The quantities asked below belong to that crack-tip field. Where a value at a finite distance is asked, it means the crack-tip field continued to that distance, not the full stress there.

G is the energy release rate (energy released per unit area of crack advance along the interface).

4. What to report

  1. Load case A: G_E2_h_over_P2 = G E2 h/P²; amplitude_sqrt_h_over_P = A √h/P, where A = limr→0 √(2πr) |T(r)|; phase_at_h_deg: the phase angle arg T of the crack-tip field at r = h.
  2. Load case B: G_E2_h3_over_M2 = G E2 h³/M²; amplitude_h32_over_M = A h3/2/M; phase_at_h_deg, as in case A.
  3. phase_at_h_both_deg: the same phase angle when both loads act together with M = P h (P > 0).
  4. phase_change_per_decade_deg: for load case A, the change of the phase angle arg T of the crack-tip field when r increases tenfold. Report 0 if it does not change.

Phase angles in degrees, −180° < angle ≤ 180°.

The energy release rates and the amplitudes pass if they are within 1% of the reference value; each phase angle at r = h passes if it is within 0.5° of the reference value; the change per decade passes if it is within 0.2° of the reference value.

Step 1 How much energy does the crack release?

Background: the energy release rate, plane strain.

Far ahead of the tip the two layers are bonded and carry no stress. Far behind it each layer is a separate beam with a known force and moment. When the crack grows by a small length, the field around the tip moves along with it unchanged. So the energy release rate can be found from the two ends alone, far ahead and far behind, without the field near the tip.

From the case study:

Because the two materials differ, the stresses ahead of the tip are not those of a crack in one material: the traction σ₂₂ + iσ₁₂ carries a factor riε, so its phase turns by ε ln r as the distance r changes. The bimaterial constant ε depends on the two materials only; here it is ln(2/3)/2π = −0.0645, and the phase falls by 8.514° for every tenfold increase of r. The phase angle ψ therefore has a meaning only with a reference length; the task takes h.

What happened

The energy rejects Answer A, and it cannot judge Answer B. Answer A’s single-load angles differ by 46.2°, 1.77° short of the 47.97° that the energy fixes, so at least one of them is off, whatever the elasticity solution is. Answer B meets the energy exactly, and yet each of its three angles lies 0.85° below the case’s reference. A shift of every angle by the same amount turns both complex factors together: their sum turns with them, and the difference of the angles does not change. Neither test can see such a shift, nor a change of sign of every angle; only a computation of one absolute angle can.

Answer A is the answer of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash to the version of the task on this page, which did not give the interface-crack definitions. The case scored it 6 of 8 checks and 50 of 100 rubric points: it lost the 25 points of each single-load phase angle. GPT-6.1 Sol, Kimi K3.1 Pro and GLM-5.3 passed all eight checks, with 100 points each.

5 Take away

Leaving out textbook physics does not hide it

Without the definitions, every run wrote the oscillating crack-tip field and the bimaterial constant itself and got the turn of the phase exactly. A test of whether an agent supplies physics must use physics it does not already carry. That physics is step 2 of this page, which the task did not state.

An exact relation is the cheapest test of a computed number

The energy fixes the size of the difference of the two phase angles without any computation. One run stated it; DeepSeek's answers miss it by 1.8°, which shows their error before any comparison with a reference. Step 4 derived that relation from the beams, and Section 4 used it.

Where the field turns, read it at the tip

The phase of an interface crack's field changes with distance, so a reading has to be extrapolated to the tip and moved to the reference length. Averaging near the tip on a short model cost DeepSeek one degree. The explorer shows how far the phase moves between r = h/1000 and r = h.

A printed value is not a reference by itself

Two computations of ours and the agents' own agree with each other and differ from the paper's printed table at this point. The benchmark rests on the computations, and the difference is stated, not resolved. Answer B passed the energy test, which fixes the difference of the angles, not the angles themselves.

From the case study: “Each lesson rests on eight runs of one problem in two versions: observations to test on new problems, not measured rates.” The full case study also has the other three runs, a first version of the task that stated the interface-crack definitions, and all the grading.
