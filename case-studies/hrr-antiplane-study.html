Extra exercise: how much do the faces of a tearing crack slip?

A material that hardens needs more stress as its strain increases. Near a sharp crack, that curved stress-strain relation changes how stress and strain rise towards the tip. You will find the relative slip of the faces and the distance at which a given strain is reached, then test the energy calculation that fixes their size.

You will practise
  • finding how stress and strain vary with distance from a crack tip;
  • separating the shape of a field from its size;
  • choosing a check that can actually catch a wrong calculation.
You need
  • stress intensity factors and energy release rate;
  • gradients, polar coordinates and ordinary differential equations;
  • pen and paper, and something to evaluate an integral numerically.

1 The problem

This is an extra exercise to the HRR study page. The HRR field proper is the tensile (mode I) crack-tip field in a hardening material, found by Hutchinson and by Rice and Rosengren in 1968. Here the same method is applied to a crack in antiplane shear (mode III), where every step has a closed form, so you can do the whole calculation yourself.

A body contains a straight slit along the negative x axis, with its tip at the origin. It is loaded in antiplane shear, also called tearing or mode III: the only displacement, w(x, y), is along z, out of the plane of the picture, and the two faces slide past each other along the crack front. The displacement is an odd function of y, meaning that reversing y reverses the sign of w. The faces are traction-free, meaning that no force per unit area acts on them.

The hardening exponent n is the power that controls this relation; in this convention, a larger n means less stress increase for a given strain increase.

J has unit N/mm because it is energy per unit crack advance per unit thickness: energy in N mm divided by an advance in mm and a thickness in mm gives N/mm.

Here ds is a small length along the contour. For this power-law field, J is path independent: changing the contour without crossing the tip does not change J. We use a circle traversed in increasing θ, from the lower face at −π, through the region ahead of the tip, to the upper face at +π.

Find the full relative slip of the faces, Δw = w(r, +π) − w(r, −π), at r = 0.0200 mm, and the distance ahead of the tip, at θ = 0, where |γ| = 0.0100.

2 The physical picture

Before calculating the slip, look at where the strain is concentrated. The three curves each join points with the same shear strain magnitude. Drag the hardening-exponent slider to change their shape. At n = 1 they are circles centred on the tip; as n grows they shift ahead, approaching circles that pass through the tip.

Stress varies as r−1/(n+1); the magnitude of this exponent is 0.2222.

Strain varies as r−n/(n+1); the magnitude of this exponent is 0.7778.

  1. The faces slide, rather than open. The upper face moves out of the picture and the lower face moves into it. Spatial changes in that displacement give the shear strain.
  2. The ideal sharp tip concentrates strain. The same nonzero energy flux must pass through circles of smaller and smaller radius. On a circle of radius r, the contour length is proportional to r, so J scales as W r; because J is the same for every r, W scales as 1/r. This argument uses a field whose angular shape stays the same as its radius changes. The field is singular, meaning that its stress and strain grow without bound as distance tends to zero in this ideal model.
  3. The material law shares that rise between stress and strain. A larger n gives a weaker stress singularity and a stronger strain singularity. Their product always varies inversely with distance. The strain also concentrates more strongly ahead of the crack, as the contours show.
  4. J sets how big the field is. The material law and the free faces determine the distance dependence and angular shape. The supplied energy flux then fixes the size of the displacement, stress and strain.

The picture separates the calculation into three parts: the power of distance in step 1, the shape around the tip in step 2, and the size fixed by J in step 3. Step 4 uses those parts to find the slip and the equal-strain distance.

Step 1 How singular is the field?

Background: energy release rate, the J integral, the power-law material.

J is the same on every circle around the tip. For a field whose angular shape does not change with radius, this means that energy density times radius must stay constant. The first term inside the brackets is the energy term, from W Nx. Expanding the traction term shows its two parts explicitly:

The negative traction part comes from −τrzγr cos θ. The positive traction part comes from +τrzγθ sin θ; this is the cross term containing f f′ sin θ.

Since w = a rλf(θ) is a length and f is dimensionless, a has units of length to the power 1 − λ. Thus ap has units of length, and κapI has units (N/mm²)(mm) = N/mm, as J must.

The factor 2 is justified by parity, the behaviour of a function when its argument changes sign. An even function keeps its value when the argument changes sign; an odd function changes its sign. Here f is odd, its derivative f′ is even, and q is even. In particular, ff′ sin θ is odd times even times odd, so it is even. Contributions from the lower and upper halves therefore add. That cross term does not cancel.

A short program, if you want one 
import numpy as np
n = 3.5
lam = 1/(n + 1); p = 1 + 1/n
psi = np.linspace(0, np.pi/2, 2001)          # the parameter of step 2
D = np.cos(psi)**2 + n**2*np.sin(psi)**2
theta = psi + np.arctan2(n*np.sin(psi), np.cos(psi))
q = D**(-n/(2*(n + 1)))
f, fp = q*np.sin(psi)/lam, q*np.cos(psi)      # f and f'
g = (n/(n + 1)*q**p*np.cos(theta)             # energy term
     - q**(p - 2)*lam**2*f**2*np.cos(theta)   # traction term, part 1
     + q**(p - 2)*lam*f*fp*np.sin(theta))     # traction term, part 2 (the cross term)
I = 2*np.sum((g[1:] + g[:-1])/2*np.diff(theta))   # trapezoid rule, upper half, doubled
print(f[-1], I)                               # f(pi) and I

It prints f(pi) = 1.698 and I = 2.0196.

What happened

Both pass Test 1, recovering J = 0.120 N/mm exactly with their own routines. Test 2 separates them: A gets π = 3.142, while B gets π/2 = 1.571. The derivative of an odd function is even. Extending both f and f′ as odd removes a cross term that should remain.

For the task material, B obtains I = 1.3215 instead of 2.0196. Its J coefficient, κI, is 624.1 instead of 953.9. Fitting the same J with that smaller coefficient makes the amplitude too large. It would give 1.833 µm for the slip and 71.67 µm for the distance ahead.

Agent B was the second DeepSeek run, called M20 in the case study. The case study describes it as follows:

M20 (V1, no knowledge, second repeat) set up the antiplane problem correctly and found the exponent 2/9. Building the full-circle J, it extended f′ as odd, like f, instead of even. That cancelled a cross term: its J coefficient was 624.1 instead of 953.9, and every number shifted, the slips by 39%, the stresses by 10% and the contour radii by 53%. Its energy check recovered the input J with the same coefficient, so it could not see the error, and the answer stated that all seven numbers were within tolerance. 6 Take away

The exponent is the easy part

The energy scaling fixes the crack exponent, but not the amplitude. Here both runs found the right exponent; losing a term in J still shifted every requested number. Check the calculation that sets the size, not only the power of distance.

Checks that reuse the same step are not independent

Recomputing J with the coefficient used to fit the amplitude checks consistency, not whether that coefficient is right. These checking methods share the very step that might be wrong. The known elastic limit tests the disputed cross term without reusing the fitted amplitude.

These lessons are based on a few runs of problems built from one paper. Treat them as ideas to test on new problems, not as measured error rates. The full case study has the other problems, all runs and the grading.
