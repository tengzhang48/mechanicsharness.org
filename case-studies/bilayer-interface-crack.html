Fracture mechanics case study · AI agents and an interface crack · Runs of 14 November 2026

An interface crack between two elastic layers: can AI agents find the mode mix without being told how an interface crack behaves?

Read the mechanics: model and solutionSee how the agents did

Suo and Hutchinson (1990) solved a crack on the interface of two bonded elastic layers under a force and a moment. Its energy release rate follows from beam theory; its phase angle, how much of the loading is opening and how much shear, needs the elasticity solution, and an interface crack has a crack-tip field whose phase turns with the distance from the tip. We gave the problem to four AI agents without the paper, without the web, and without saying how an interface crack behaves. Three answered all eight numbers within tolerance; DeepSeek missed two phase angles by 0.65° and 1.12°. All four brought in the interface-crack physics themselves and computed the phase angles with finite elements of their own.

Aim

To see whether an agent supplies the special physics of an interface crack when the task does not state it, and whether it can compute a quantity that has no closed form.

Teng Zhang · record of 14 November 2026. One run each of GPT-6.1 Sol (Codex CLI), Kimi K3.1 Pro, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM-5.3 (opencode), each at its highest reasoning setting, in a container with Python, NumPy and SciPy, a three-hour limit and web tools off; and, earlier the same day, one run each of a first version that stated the interface-crack definitions (Section 11). The numbers were checked by a program against our reference; the method notes were read by a second model (Grok 4.7) and by us. No expert has reviewed this note. The page states the reference: the benchmark is published with its answer.

Our question
Given only the two layers, the crack and the loads, can an AI agent find the energy release rate, the amplitude of the crack-tip field and its phase at r = h, and how that phase turns with r?
Posed unchanged to every run. The paper, the interface-crack definitions and the reference were withheld, and web tools were switched off.
The answer and its present limit

Three runs of four did, and the fourth had the physics right. GPT-6.1 Sol, Kimi K3.1 Pro and GLM-5.3 passed all eight checks; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash passed six, its two single-load phase angles off by 0.65° and 1.12° through the way it read them from its solution. Every run wrote the crack-tip field of an interface crack itself and got the turn of the phase exactly.

The phase angles rest on finite elements: two computations of ours and the agents' own, which agree with each other and differ from the source paper's printed table at this point by 0.85°. The difference is not explained. Each result is one run.

What the agents did · what separated them · why it matters

What the agents did

All four took the energy release rate from the beams, wrote the crack-tip field of an interface crack with its bimaterial constant, built a finite-element model of the two layers and read the complex stress intensity factor from it.

What separated them

Only the accuracy of that reading. DeepSeek averaged the phase close to the tip on a short model; the other three extrapolated it and refined their meshes. An exact test from the energy, which needs no computation, shows the error at once.

Why it matters

The physics the task left out is physics these agents carry: they wrote it in standard notation before computing anything, then checked it. A harder test would need a crack-tip field that is not in the textbooks.

1 · The model

Two bonded layers with a crack on their interface

Two isotropic elastic layers, each infinitely long, are bonded along a straight interface, in plane strain. The upper layer is nine times stiffer than the lower and half as thick. A semi-infinite crack lies on the interface, its faces free of traction; ahead of its tip the layers are perfectly bonded.

The loading is applied far behind the tip, where each layer is a separate beam. The upper layer carries a compressive force P and a bending moment M that bends it away from the lower layer; the lower layer carries the force and moment that leave the bonded part far ahead of the tip free of stress. Two load cases are asked, the force alone and the moment alone, and their combination with M = Ph.

The task asks for the energy release rate, the amplitude of the singular traction ahead of the tip, the phase of that traction's crack-tip field at the distance h, and how that phase changes when the distance grows tenfold, with “report 0 if it does not change”. It does not say that the phase changes, nor give any formula for the crack-tip field.

As in the paper The geometry, the loading and the quantities are those of Suo and Hutchinson (1990). The parameters (E₁ = 9E₂, ν₁ = ν₂ = 1/3, h/H = 0.5) are our choice, at a point of the paper's printed table.

2 · The solution

The energy from the beams; the phase from the elasticity solution

The energy release rate needs only the beams. Far ahead of the tip the bonded layers carry no stress; far behind, each is a beam with a known force and moment. When the crack advances, a length of bonded, unstressed bilayer becomes two loaded beams, so G is the elastic energy per unit length of the two beams, with the plane-strain moduli Ē = E/(1 − ν²). Both layers count.

An interface crack has its own crack-tip field. Because the two materials differ, the stresses ahead of the tip are not those of a crack in one material: the traction σ₂₂ + iσ₁₂ carries a factor riε, so its phase turns by ε ln r as the distance r changes. The bimaterial constant ε depends on the two materials only; here it is ln(2/3)/2π = −0.0645, and the phase falls by 8.514° for every tenfold increase of r. The phase angle ψ therefore has a meaning only with a reference length; the task takes h.

The amplitude follows from G. For two materials, G and |K| are related through cosh²(πε), which equals 25/24 here; the relation of one material would give |K| 2.0% too low.

The phase angle has no closed form. It needs the solution of the elastic problem, which the paper obtained from an integral equation and tabulated. Our reference comes from two finite-element computations that share nothing but the problem: other elements, meshes and codes, and other ways of reading K (an interaction integral and the tractions ahead of the tip; the crack opening behind it). They agree within 0.02°: 52.249° and 52.268° under the force, 4.279° and 4.271° under the moment.

One relation is exact. K is linear in the loads and G is quadratic, so the energy alone fixes the size of the difference of the two phase angles: 47.97° (the cosine of the difference is fixed, so its sign follows from the computed factors). Only one absolute phase has to be computed, and the second computation meets this difference to 10⁻⁵°. Under both loads the two complex factors add.

Checked The closed forms were derived again by hand in a review (GPT-6.1 Sol) before it read our key, and equal ours to fifteen digits. Each finite-element computation was checked against exact cases: one material with equal thicknesses (exact 49.107°) and a crack on the interface of two half-planes.

3 · What the solution says

The force shears the interface; the moment opens it

Under the force alone the phase angle is 52.25°: the shear part of the crack-tip loading is larger than the opening part. Under the moment alone it is 4.28°: almost pure opening. With M = Ph the two complex factors add and the phase angle is 30.43°. A delaminating layer that is pushed shears its interface; one that is peeled opens it.

The phase angle is zero, pure opening at r = h, only if the upper layer is pulled instead of pushed, with a moment 12.57 times |P|h that bends it away; and even then only at r = h, because the phase turns with the distance.

Our reference and the printed table differ. At this point the paper's Table 3 prints 51.4°, 0.85° below our 52.25°. Our first computation agrees with the printed tables at thickness ratio 1 and in the thin-layer limit; both computations differ from them at ratios 0.5 and 0.1, for one material too. The same authors' later formula for one material agrees with our computations within 0.4°. We have not solved the paper's integral equation and have not asked the authors; we state this as a disagreement between our computations and a printed table, not as an error.

4 · The test

The problem the agents received

Each run received one file, the task below, in a container with Python 3.12, NumPy and SciPy, two processors and a limit of three hours that the agent was not told. Web tools were off. The prompt asked it to solve the problem in its folder, without the web, and to end with what remained unverified.

Eight numbers were checked: the energy release rates and amplitudes within 1%, each phase angle within 0.5°, the turn per decade within 0.2°. Points follow a rubric approved on 14 November: 5 for the turn of the phase, 20 for the two energy release rates, 15 for the two amplitudes, 25 for each single-load phase angle and 10 for the combination; a piece counts only if all its numbers pass.

A second model, Grok 4.7, read each run's files without running anything and answered three questions: where the phase angles come from, whether the checks the run claims are in its files, and where the first error is.

The task as given
# An interface crack between two elastic layers

Solve the problem below and report the requested numbers in `results.json`. The problem is fully stated here; the
method is your choice. Python 3 with NumPy and SciPy is available. Work only inside this folder.

## 1. The bilayer and the crack

- Plane strain. Two isotropic, linear elastic layers, each infinitely long in the x<sub>1</sub> direction, are bonded
  along the interface x<sub>2</sub> = 0. Small strains, no body forces.
- **Layer 1** (above, 0 < x<sub>2</sub> < h): Young's modulus E<sub>1</sub> = 9 E<sub>2</sub>, Poisson's ratio
  ν<sub>1</sub> = 1/3.
- **Layer 2** (below, −H < x<sub>2</sub> < 0): Young's modulus E<sub>2</sub>, Poisson's ratio ν<sub>2</sub> = 1/3.
- Thickness ratio **h/H = 0.5**.
- The outer surfaces x<sub>2</sub> = h and x<sub>2</sub> = −H are traction-free.
- A semi-infinite crack lies on the interface along x<sub>1</sub> < 0, with its tip at the origin. The layers are
  perfectly bonded for x<sub>1</sub> > 0. The crack faces are traction-free; ignore any contact between them.

## 2. The loading

The bilayer is loaded only far behind the crack tip (x<sub>1</sub> → −∞), where each layer is a separate beam. There
the stresses are σ<sub>12</sub> = σ<sub>22</sub> = 0 and

- in layer 1:   σ<sub>11</sub> = −P/h − 12 M (x<sub>2</sub> − h/2)/h³,
- in layer 2:   σ<sub>11</sub> = P/H + 12 (M + P (h + H)/2)(x<sub>2</sub> + H/2)/H³.

So layer 1 carries an axial compressive force P and a bending moment M (positive M bends layer 1 away from layer 2),
and the stresses in layer 2 are those that leave the bonded part far ahead of the tip (x<sub>1</sub> → +∞)
stress-free. Forces and moments are per unit length in the x<sub>3</sub> direction.

- **Load case A:** P > 0, M = 0.
- **Load case B:** P = 0, M > 0.

## 3. The crack-tip field

Write the tractions on the interface a distance r ahead of the tip (x<sub>1</sub> = r > 0, x<sub>2</sub> = 0) as one
complex number, T(r) = σ<sub>22</sub> + i σ<sub>12</sub>. As r → 0 the tractions are dominated by the singular
crack-tip field. The quantities asked below belong to that crack-tip field. Where a value at a finite distance is
asked, it means the crack-tip field continued to that distance, not the full stress there.

G is the energy release rate (energy released per unit area of crack advance along the interface).

## 4. What to report

1. Load case A: `G_E2_h_over_P2` = G E<sub>2</sub> h/P²; `amplitude_sqrt_h_over_P` = A √h/P, where
   A = lim<sub>r→0</sub> √(2πr) |T(r)|; `phase_at_h_deg`: the phase angle arg T of the crack-tip field at r = h.
2. Load case B: `G_E2_h3_over_M2` = G E<sub>2</sub> h³/M²; `amplitude_h32_over_M` = A h<sup>3/2</sup>/M;
   `phase_at_h_deg`, as in case A.
3. `phase_at_h_both_deg`: the same phase angle when both loads act together with M = P h (P > 0).
4. `phase_change_per_decade_deg`: for load case A, the change of the phase angle arg T of the crack-tip field when r
   increases tenfold. Report 0 if it does not change.

Phase angles in degrees, −180° < angle ≤ 180°.

## 5. What to deliver

- `results.json` in this folder, in exactly this form (numbers as JSON numbers):

```
{
  "caseA": {"G_E2_h_over_P2": 0.0, "amplitude_sqrt_h_over_P": 0.0, "phase_at_h_deg": 0.0},
  "caseB": {"G_E2_h3_over_M2": 0.0, "amplitude_h32_over_M": 0.0, "phase_at_h_deg": 0.0},
  "phase_at_h_both_deg": 0.0,
  "phase_change_per_decade_deg": 0.0
}
```

- `method.md`: how you obtained the numbers, the assumptions you made, and the checks you ran.
- Any code you used (including for checks), in this folder.

Each reported number is compared with a reference value. The energy release rates and the amplitudes pass if they are
within 1% of the reference value; each phase angle at r = h passes if it is within 0.5° of the reference value; the
change per decade passes if it is within 0.2° of the reference value.
What was asked, abridged

For the force alone and the moment alone: G, the amplitude lim √(2πr)|σ₂₂ + iσ₁₂| and the phase of the crack-tip field at r = h; the phase under both loads with M = Ph; and the change of that phase when r increases tenfold, 0 if it does not change.

7 · DeepSeek V4.1 Flash

The physics confirmed, the phase read too close to the tip

The run did the most to confirm the crack-tip field: it solved the two-material tip problem as a determinant, which vanishes at the exponent −1/2 ± iε, and calibrated its crack-opening reading on a problem whose exact solution is that field. Its energy release rates, amplitudes and turn per decade are right.

Its two single-load phase angles, 51.6° and 5.4°, are 0.65° and 1.12° off. Its note says the phase was “extrapolated linearly in r” and that the meshes agree “within about one degree”. The grader, reading its code, places the first error in the numerics: by that reading the phase was averaged over distances from 6 × 10⁻⁵ to 2 × 10⁻³ of h rather than extrapolated, on a model reaching 6h behind and ahead of the tip. We have not rerun its code. Its phase under both loads, 30.6°, passes although it is built from the two that fail.

10 · Where the physics came from

Written from memory, then checked

No run searched the web. Web tools were off, and no log shows a use of the network other than the model. One run, DeepSeek, ran a single search of the container's system folders for files mentioning “suo”, “interface crack” or “hutchinson”; it found only unrelated system files and opened none.

Every run wrote the interface-crack relations at once. The crack-tip field with riε, the bimaterial constant in the Dundurs form and the relation between G and |K| for two materials (with cosh²(πε), or its equal 1 − β²) appear in each note in standard notation, before any computation and with no source. GLM is the exception for the energy relation, which it gives as “derived and verified” on its own crack-tip field.

Every run then checked what it had written against something of its own: the slope of the phase in its solution, the magnitude from the opening against the energy, the tip problem as a determinant, the energy of a computed tip field.

Our reading. These relations are reproduced in textbooks and reviews, and the models write them as they are written there. That they come from the models' training is our inference; the files show only that they were written without derivation. The phase angle at these parameters is the one thing a model could have recalled from the literature, from the printed table; no run reported 51.4°. In the trapped-front case of the same week, the result needed was a less reproduced one, and three of four runs replaced it by a wrong form.

13 · Lessons

What this case study teaches, and what it does not

Each lesson rests on eight runs of one problem in two versions: observations to test on new problems, not measured rates.

Domain knowledge

Leaving out textbook physics does not hide it

Without the definitions, every run wrote the oscillating crack-tip field and the bimaterial constant itself and got the turn of the phase exactly. A test of whether an agent supplies physics must use physics it does not already carry.

The four method notes of this version (Sections 5 to 8).

Verification

A recalled formula can be checked by the computation it serves

Each run tested the relation it had written against its own solution: the slope of the phase, the magnitude from the opening, the tip problem, the energy of a computed field. None of those checks needed the paper.

Checks sections of the method notes.

Verification

An exact relation is the cheapest test of a computed number

The energy fixes the size of the difference of the two phase angles without any computation. One run stated it; DeepSeek's answers miss it by 1.8°, which shows their error before any comparison with a reference.

Section 9; GPT-6.1 Sol's method note.

Numerics

Where the field turns, read it at the tip

The phase of an interface crack's field changes with distance, so a reading has to be extrapolated to the tip and moved to the reference length. Averaging near the tip on a short model cost DeepSeek one degree.

The grade of the DeepSeek run; its method note.

Reference

A printed value is not a reference by itself

Two computations of ours and the agents' own agree with each other and differ from the paper's printed table at this point. The benchmark rests on the computations, and the difference is stated, not resolved.

Section 3; the benchmark notes.

What the study does not show: that the three runs that passed would pass again, that DeepSeek would miss again, or how the agents would do on an interface crack whose crack-tip field is not in the literature. Each would need further runs.

Study basis

One problem in two versions, four runs of each, one per model, through two agent programs: a case study, not a ranking. The reference comes from our computations, with the paper's theory; the readings of the runs come from an AI model and from us. No expert in fracture mechanics has reviewed the benchmark or this note, and the authors of the paper have not been contacted.

The agents' words quoted here come from files they saved, their method notes. Their private reasoning was not used.

Where this case departs from our usual practice: the version on this page was written after the first version's results, and run the same day; the rubric was approved between the two; the problem statement was reviewed by one AI model, not yet by an outside expert.

Appendix B · Execution records

Execution records

Web tools. For the three opencode runs the web fetch and web search actions were denied for every agent and sub-agent. For Codex its web search and the account's connectors were both switched off. The network itself stayed on, because the models are reached through it. No log shows a use of it other than the model.

Paths outside the folder. One DeepSeek command searched the container's system folders, as described in Section 10; its other paths outside the folder are the output files of its own background commands. No other run named a path outside its folder.

Grading. Grok 4.7 read each run through opencode with every action denied except reading and searching inside its folder, from one file that holds every text file of the run. Each of the four runs of this version was graded at the first attempt. The tool derives the summary of the second question from the checks the grader lists.

Timing. The four runs of this version were launched within one minute of each other and ended by themselves. Minutes are the span of each run's log; for Codex, from launch to end.
