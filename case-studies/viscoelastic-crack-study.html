A guided fracture mechanics exercise. Find the energy a strip can release and the fastest steady crack, then test where a steady branch ends.

How fast can a crack run steadily through a viscoelastic strip?

A slow crack in a viscoelastic material needs only the intrinsic fracture energy; for a fast crack the material responds stiffly and dissipates more, so the crack needs up to G0/G∞ times as much. Here G0/G∞ is the ratio of the material’s stiffness under fast loading to its stiffness after it relaxes. In a strip, the energy the specimen can release is capped. When that cap lies below the classical high-speed value, steady growth ends at a finite terminal speed, and you will find it.

You will practise
  • using energy to bound what a specimen can supply;
  • separating what a material’s memory changes from what it leaves alone;
  • finding where a steady solution stops existing, from the physics.
You need
  • antiplane shear, the energy release rate and the Dugdale cohesive zone from the introductory page;
  • basic complex numbers: an analytic function, and the arctangent of an imaginary number;
  • pen and paper, and a calculator. The page supplies the two results that need a computer: a short table of one function and one zone length.

1 The problem

An infinite strip of height 2h deforms in antiplane shear: the only displacement w is out of the plane. Its edges are held at w = ±Δ. A crack runs along the midline at constant speed v; in the frame moving with it the fields are steady. Steady means that, seen from this moving frame, nothing changes in time. Ahead of the front the interface is intact; behind it a cohesive zone of unknown length lc carries a constant shear traction τc until the opening 2w reaches δc; behind that the crack is traction free. In antiplane shear the opening is the jump 2w in out-of-plane displacement across the crack line: the two faces slide past each other.

h is the half-height of the strip. τc and δc are the cohesive traction and the critical opening. G∞ is the relaxed shear modulus, and tc is the creep time defined below. Their numerical values are not needed for the dimensionless answers.

The material. The material is a standard linear solid: a spring G∞ in parallel with a Maxwell branch, so it responds with the instantaneous modulus G0 to fast loading and relaxes to G∞. A Maxwell branch is a second spring, of modulus G0 − G∞, in series with a dashpot, a damper whose force grows with its rate of extension. Under fast loading the dashpot has no time to move, so both springs carry the load. Under a load held for a long time the dashpot gives way, and only the spring G∞ is left. The creep time tc sets how long this takes. It appears in the creep compliance, the shear strain at time t per unit shear stress applied at time 0:

λ is at most 1. Here λ = 0.1, so the material is ten times stiffer under fast loading than after it relaxes. See the standard solid and its creep time.

The cohesive zone. The joined halves separate by the Dugdale law of the introductory page. Nothing slides until the shear traction reaches τc. The traction then stays at τc while the faces slide apart, until the opening reaches δc. There the traction drops to zero. The work done on the zone per unit area of new crack is the intrinsic fracture energy Γ0 = τcδc. In the upper half of the strip, the conditions are:

τ1 and τ2 are the shear stresses on vertical and horizontal planes, and γ1, γ2 are the matching shear strains. See the sign conventions and traction.

Why speed matters. In the classical picture, the energy needed to drive the crack rises with speed along an S-shaped curve, from Γ0 at slow speed to (G0/G∞)Γ0 at high speed. See why the curve rises. This problem asks what changes when the crack runs through a strip of finite height.

Material crossing the zone responds as:

  1. The memory length. In one creep time the crack moves a distance vtc, called the memory length. A material point remembers, roughly, the stresses it felt over the last creep time, and so over the last memory length of its path.
  2. A slow crack meets relaxed material. A point near the crack line takes a time lc/v = (ε/V)tc to cross the cohesive zone. When that time is long compared with tc, the material relaxes as it crosses and behaves as the soft solid G∞.
  3. A fast crack meets stiff material. When the crossing time is short, the material near the zone responds as the stiff solid G0. It relaxes only far behind the zone, over a distance of the order of the memory length. In this strip, though, the zone lengthens as the crack speeds up, so the crossing time never falls below about 0.63 creep times (its smallest value, near V = 8.9). The material crossing the zone is never fully unrelaxed here.
  4. Energy is lost while the material relaxes. The dashpot carries the stress that the relaxed spring does not, and it dissipates energy at a rate set by that stress. At low speed the loss is concentrated near the zone; as the speed rises, it spreads over a large region behind the zone.

The last meter names the response of material crossing the zone: relaxed (soft) when the crossing takes more than 3 creep times, unrelaxed (stiff) when it takes less than 0.3, and partly relaxed in between. These limits are a rough guide chosen for this page, not sharp boundaries. Step 2 shows that, for a given zone length, the stress does not depend on the speed. Step 4 shows how the memory enters through the opening of the zone. Step 5 finds the fastest steady crack.

Step 2 Why is the stress field the elastic one?

Background: antiplane shear, traction, the creep compliance.

In a steady state, every material point on a horizontal line, a streamline, meets the same stress history, shifted in time. Its present strain is a fixed linear rule applied to that history: a weighted sum of the stresses it has felt. The rule is the same at every height and at every position along the line. Equilibrium involves stress only. Compatibility says that both strains come from one displacement w, so γ1,y = γ2,x. The history rule can be undone, and it commutes with both derivatives. So compatibility of strain becomes the same condition on stress:

The two conditions on stress are the Cauchy-Riemann equations, the conditions for a complex function to be analytic. T is the complex shear stress in units of τc.

From the case study:

In this steady problem the stress is curl free as well as divergence free, so Hui and Long write it as one analytic function T(Z) of the complex coordinate, on the branch analytic in the upper half strip.

The boundary conditions of this stress problem are all conditions on stress:

  • τ2 = τc in the cohesive zone, and τ2 = 0 on the open crack;
  • τ1 = 0 on the intact interface, because w = 0 there at all times, so γ1 = 0 and hence τ1 = 0;
  • τ1 = 0 on the edges, because w = Δ there at all times.

5 Find the error

Two agents solved this problem with the same first steps. Both reduced the bulk to a harmonic problem (Laplace’s equation) with a history convolution (the weighted stress history of step 4). They differ in where the steady branch ends. Agent A found the endpoint from the separation condition, as in step 5. Agent B worked with a transform along the crack line. Its method needs a function of the transform variable, called the symbol, to be split into two factors. That split works only while a pole of the symbol, a point where it becomes infinite, lies below the edge of a strip in the plane of the transform variable. Agent B took the speed at which the pole reaches that edge as the end of the steady branch.

What happened

A steady branch ends where the separation condition can no longer be met, and only Agent A passes both tests. At V = 10 the zone can still open by more than δc, so a finite steady state exists, and Agent B ruled it out. Agent B’s endpoint is fixed by λ alone, so it cannot move with the critical opening.

Agent B took the limit of its factorization, a property of its method, for a limit of the physics. The number 1/λ also appears in the classical limit R → G0/G∞, but there it is a ratio of energies, not a speed.

6 Take away

Existence comes from the physics, not the transform

Where a factorization stops being analytic is a property of the method. The steady branch ends where the separation condition can no longer be met. Here that condition ties the terminal speed to the critical opening as well as to λ.

The stress is elastic; memory enters through the opening

In the steady strip, the stress is that of an elastic strip with the same zone length. The material’s memory changes the strain, and through it the opening of the zone. The speed selects the state only through the separation condition.

The specimen can cap the toughness

The far-ahead stress cannot reach the cohesive strength, so R < 1/δc. The classical high-speed value G0/G∞ is reached only if δc ≤ λ. Otherwise the steady branch ends first, at a terminal speed.

These lessons are based on a few runs of one problem. Treat them as ideas to test on new problems, not as measured error rates.
