Mixed-mode crack fronts and trapping

A straight crack may be described by three stress intensity factors. A curved, three-dimensional front adds two further questions: how the modes interact along the front, and where growth is resisted.

20 · Mixed-mode curved fronts21 · Trapping and stabilityReview loading modes

20. Why is mixed-mode fracture harder for a curved front?

Imagine a planar crack in the x–z plane, with the front on a line approximately parallel to z and advancing in the x direction. Opening normal to the plane is Mode I. Sliding in the plane and across the front is Mode II. Sliding in the plane and along the front is Mode III.

For a homogeneous isotropic linear elastic solid, away from free surfaces and with small-scale yielding, the local energy release rate is

G = (KI² + KII²) / E′ + KIII² / (2μ)

Here E′ = E/(1 − ν²) and μ = E/[2(1 + ν)]. A stress intensity factor has units of stress times the square root of length. G has units of energy per newly created crack area. The simple sum above must not be used unchanged for anisotropic, bimaterial or nonlinear crack-tip fields.

The detail that a two-dimensional picture misses

For a straight front, Mode II and Mode III represent different elastic shear responses. A wavy front makes the fields vary along the front, and the local tangent and advance direction change. Consequently, shear modes can interact. Simply copying the Mode I perturbation rule for both shears, or just rotating the far-field stress intensity factors, is not generally a valid three-dimensional elastic calculation.

Even with small waviness, the first-order change in the crack-tip field can contain a nonlocal response to changes elsewhere along the front, as well as local orientation terms. The coefficients of those effects depend on geometry and elastic constitutive assumptions. They are not provided here; deriving or computing them is part of the mechanics problem.

Try it. With E = 100 GPa, ν = 0.25 and (KI, KII, KIII) = (2, 1, 1) MPa√m, calculate G.

Show the working

E′ = 106.667 GPa, μ = 40 GPa. The opening and in-plane shear terms contribute 46.875 J/m². Mode III contributes 12.5 J/m². Thus G = 59.375 J/m². This checks the local energy relation, not the change of K caused by a curved front.

21. Why can tough regions trap a crack front?

A crack front encounters different local resistance as it passes through a heterogeneous material. Write the local fracture resistance as Γ(z) and the available elastic energy as G(z), where the front coordinate is z. At an actively advancing point in quasistatic, rate-independent fracture, G = Γ is a necessary condition. A pinned point may have G < Γ.

Why a curved front is a collective object

Advancing one portion of a front changes elastic loading elsewhere along it. That redistribution is generally nonlocal. The language of line tension is a useful analogy for resistance to roughness, but a constant local line-tension law is not the exact first-order response of a three-dimensional elastic crack. A quantitative prediction requires an appropriate elasticity solution or a controlled approximation.

Finding a shape for which the moving segments satisfy G = Γ does not automatically prove that the shape is stable. Stability depends on nearby admissible front perturbations, the surrounding elasticity and whether force or displacement is controlled. Large deflections, kinks, contact, strong material contrast or finite process zones may invalidate a small-perturbation model.

A sensible sequence

  1. Identify the crack plane, loading and local Mode I/II/III basis.
  2. Decide whether isotropic linear elasticity and a slightly perturbed front are justified.
  3. Compute how changing the front affects the local stress intensity factors and energy release.
  4. Combine the elastic driving forces with a stated toughness or growth law.
  5. Test equilibrium and stability separately, and compare against another solution route where possible.

Think about it. A numerical solution gives G(z) = Γ(z) along a trial wavy front. Is that enough to claim a stable trapped configuration?

Show the reasoning

No. This equality is a local equilibrium condition for advancing portions of a quasistatic front. Stability requires considering nearby admissible shapes and the loading system; a solution may also be outside the domain where the chosen perturbation approximation is valid.

For researchers: the classical analysis of planar first-order crack-front perturbations is associated with Rice (1985), and the mixed-mode shear extension with Gao and Rice (1986). Their theory supplies specialized response coefficients. This lesson deliberately gives the conceptual tools, not the coefficients or answers to the MechanicsHarness trapped-front benchmark.
