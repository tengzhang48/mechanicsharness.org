A guided fracture mechanics exercise. Find the distance dependence by hand, use angular values supplied by a numerical calculation, then check the energy calculation that fixes the amplitude.

How strongly does stress concentrate at a crack in a metal that hardens?

A material that hardens needs more stress as its strain increases. Near a sharp crack, that curved stress-strain relation changes how stress and strain rise towards the tip. You will study an opening crack, also called mode I, and find the stress ahead of its tip and the strain energy around it.

You will practise
  • finding how stress and strain vary with distance from a crack tip;
  • separating the shape of a field from its amplitude, the coefficient that sets its size;
  • choosing a check that can actually catch a wrong energy calculation.
You need
  • the elastic crack-tip field and energy release rate from the introductory page;
  • stress and strain tensors, gradients and polar coordinates;
  • pen and paper, and a calculator for powers. The page supplies the numerical angular solution.

1 The problem

HRR names the classical mode I crack-tip field found by Hutchinson and, independently, Rice and Rosengren in 1968. A body contains a stationary sharp crack along the negative x axis, with its tip at the origin. Symmetric opening loads pull the faces apart. The faces are traction-free: no force per unit area acts on them. The body is in plane strain: displacement along z, out of the picture, is zero, and the in-plane displacements do not vary along z.

The constants α, σ0 and E only set scales. Their numerical values are not needed for the three dimensionless answers. The reference strain is ε0 = σ0/E. In polar coordinates, r is distance from the tip and θ is angle from the positive x axis. The faces are at θ = +π and −π. Ahead of the tip, θ = 0.

We assume a fully plastic total-deformation law: the current stress directly determines the total small strain εij, with no separate elastic strain added. This models the near-tip response while loading continues, not when loads are removed. The law is called J2 because it uses the squared stress deviator:

The hardening exponent n makes strain vary as the nth power of stress. In this convention, a larger n means less stress increase for a given strain increase.

J is energy per unit crack advance per unit thickness. It is positive and has the same value on every curve around the tip in this model. It fixes the field amplitude. Its contour formula comes in step 3.

Strain varies as r−n/(n+1); the magnitude of this exponent is .

  1. The crack opens. The upper and lower faces move apart within the picture. Changes in these displacements give the in-plane strains. Restraint along the crack front produces an out-of-plane stress.
  2. The ideal sharp tip concentrates stress and strain. The field is singular: stress and strain grow without bound as distance tends to zero in this model. The same nonzero J is obtained on circles of smaller and smaller radius. For a field with a fixed angular shape, this requires W to vary as 1/r.
  3. As n increases, stress becomes less singular and strain more singular, while their product still varies as 1/r.
  4. J sets the amplitude. The material law and the free faces determine the distance dependence and angular shape. J then fixes the amplitude of stress, strain and displacement.

Step 1 finds the power of distance. Step 2 supplies the angular shape. Step 3 uses the whole J integral to fix the amplitude and hence H. Step 4 integrates the energy over a disk to find C.

Step 1 How singular is the field?

Background: energy release rate, the J integral, the power-law material.

J is the same on every circle, and the contour length is proportional to radius. When the angular shape stays the same, every part of the J integrand varies as the stress-strain product. The traction-work terms do so because traction is a stress and the displacement derivative is a strain-like quantity. A nonzero, radius-independent J therefore requires energy density times radius to stay constant: W varies as 1/r.

Why the rotation of the polar basis matters

Write the polar displacements with angular functions G and L, obtained by integrating the strains. Their distance exponent is p, because a displacement gradient must have exponent p − 1 = −n/(n + 1):

Use the chain rule for the x derivative and differentiate the polar unit vectors as well. Their rotation produces the L term in the radial bracket and the G term in the angular bracket. The brackets are the dimensionless functions used in In.

Under opening symmetry, G is even and L is odd. The radial bracket is even and the angular bracket is odd, including the rotation terms. Thus the shear-work product is even.

Checks and limits

At n = 1, the same assumed law reduces to incompressible linear elasticity; no separate elastic strain is added. The exact opening field gives:

  • At n = 3, the paper’s table lists I = 5.51. The reference script gives 5.5073, consistent with that rounding. The task at n = 6.5 is solved directly, not interpolated from the table. These are numerical checks you can compare in the explorer.
  • In the reference calculation, J values on circles of different radii have a relative difference of about 3 × 10−12. This tests path independence and the displacement derivatives.
  • The field is the leading term, which dominates as distance tends to zero, of a small-strain solution without unloading. The paper warns that its singular fields are “only accurate representations of the solutions in a very small portion of the plastic zone”. The plastic zone is the region where plastic deformation is important.
  • Blunting means rounding of a tip by deformation. Finite strain means deformation large enough that geometry changes must be included. Both are outside this model, as is unloading.
  • J fixes the amplitude even when the plastic zone is not small. Computing J from a remote elastic field requires small-scale yielding: the plastic zone must be small compared with the body’s relevant dimensions. Otherwise J must come from a solution for the whole body.

Close numerical agreement checks the ideal calculation, not its accuracy for a real crack.

The following is the case study’s summary of the real run, which did not receive the notes about the paper.

Without the unit, on T1 (R31) it found p = 2/15 from the J scaling but solved a different angular problem. It imposed σrr = 0 instead of σθθ = 0 on the face, dropped the symmetry condition f‴(0) = 0, used a wrong shear compatibility relation, and dropped the shear-traction work σrθuθ,x from J as odd, although both factors are odd and the product is even. It reported H = 2.151 and C = 0.292 against 1.894 and 0.457. Its angular and Cartesian evaluations of J agreed at 7.7687; the grader found that both rested on the same wrong field and that the Cartesian check gave the shear stress the wrong parity, which repeated the dropped term. It wrote the answer before reaching the 50-minute limit.

6 Take away

The exponent is the easy part

Energy scaling fixes the crack exponent, but not the amplitude. The real run found the right exponent and still missed the requested stress and energy coefficients. Check the angular field and the whole J integral, not only the power of distance.

Agreement is not always an independent check

Angular and Cartesian evaluations can agree while repeating the same error. Ask which assumption the second calculation actually tests. The exact elastic energy coefficient tests the disputed shear term without reusing a fitted amplitude.

Check the quantity that is sensitive

H depends on the J integral through the small power 1/(n + 1). The energy coefficient C is directly proportional to its inverse. A modest error in H can therefore hide a much larger energy error. Check both, and report the individual numbers.

These lessons are based on a few runs of problems built from one paper. Treat them as ideas to test on new problems, not as measured error rates. The full case study has the other problems, all runs and the grading.
