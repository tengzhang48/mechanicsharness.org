A guided fracture mechanics exercise. Follow atoms into a stressed grain boundary, estimate how fast it relaxes, and find the crack it leaves.

How does a stressed grain boundary relax into a crack?

A thin metal film on a substrate is often in tension. Atoms on a grain boundary under tension have a lower chemical potential than atoms on the free surface, so atoms diffuse from the surface into the boundary and plate onto it as a wedge. The wedge relaxes the traction on the boundary, exponentially in time. At long times the boundary is, elastically, an edge crack of depth h with its tip at the film/substrate interface, although no free surface has been created. You will find the time scale, estimate the slowest decay rate by hand, and see why a crack is what remains.

You will practise
  • turning a diffusion law and an elastic kernel into one equation for the traction, and finding its unit of time;
  • separating variables into decaying modes, and reading relaxation times from the slowest one;
  • estimating a decay rate by hand, by giving the slowest mode the opening of a crack.
You need
  • the stress intensity factor, mode I and plane strain from the introductory page;
  • basic diffusion: a flux down the gradient of a chemical potential, and conservation of mass;
  • pen and paper, and a calculator. The one number that needs a computer, the exact decay rate, is supplied.

1 The problem

A metal film bonded to a substrate is often left strained, by epitaxy, by growth or by a thermal mismatch with the substrate (the paper’s Fig. 1). The introductory page has a film in tension on a substrate. In many films the grains reach through the whole thickness, so each grain boundary runs from the free surface down to the substrate (the paper, pp. 2865 and 2866). Even at the moderate temperatures of film processing, atoms can move between the free surface and these boundaries, and the film’s stress relaxes (the paper’s Section 1). The paper studies one such boundary in a film that cannot slide on its substrate.

The model. A polycrystalline film of thickness h lies on a substrate with the same isotropic elastic constants, so the two form an elastic half-space in plane strain. A grain boundary runs from the free surface, z = 0, to the film/substrate interface, z = h. It starts under a uniform normal tension σ₀. See plane strain.

An atom on the boundary has chemical potential −σgbΩ, lower than on the flat free surface when the boundary is in tension. Atoms therefore diffuse from the surface into the boundary and plate onto it, inserting a wedge of opening 2u(z, t). The wedge is a continuous array of climb-edge dislocations, and its elastic field, built from the solution for an edge dislocation near a free surface, lowers the traction on the boundary. Inserting a layer of material of thickness b into the boundary, from the surface down to a depth ζ, is a climb-edge dislocation of Burgers vector b at ζ (the paper, p. 2867).

At the surface the traction is zero, because the chemical potential must match the flat surface; surface diffusion is taken as fast and grooving is neglected. At the interface the film neither slides nor exchanges atoms: no flux, ∂σgb/∂z = 0, and no opening rate, ∂²σgb/∂z² = 0.

The last equation is mass conservation: atoms that stop in a stretch of boundary widen the wedge there.

Problem: stress relaxation by diffusion into a grain boundary of a thin film

A polycrystalline film of thickness h lies on a substrate with the same isotropic elastic constants (Young's modulus E, Poisson's ratio ν, E* = E/(1 − ν²)), so the two together form an elastic half-space in plane strain. The film's free surface is z = 0. A grain boundary runs perpendicular to the surface from z = 0 to the film/substrate interface at z = h. Initially the normal traction on the grain boundary is uniform, σ0 > 0 (tension).

Atoms diffuse from the free surface into the grain boundary and plate onto it. This inserts a wedge of material with opening 2u(z, t), equivalent to climb-edge dislocations on the boundary. Neither diffusion nor sliding occurs at the film/substrate interface, and the free surface stays flat (surface diffusion is fast; grooving is neglected).

    (principal value at ζ = z).
  • Diffusion. The chemical potential is μ = −σgbΩ. The atomic flux along the boundary is j = −(δgbDgb/kTΩ) ∂μ/∂z. Mass conservation gives 2 ∂u/∂t = −Ω ∂j/∂z. Here Ω is the atomic volume and δgbDgb the boundary thickness times its diffusivity.

Traction at the interface, z = h:

Traction at mid-depth:

  1. Atoms plate on the boundary. Atoms arrive along the free surface and diffuse down the boundary, toward the lower chemical potential. They plate on the boundary, and the wedge is widest near the surface.
  2. The surface relaxes first. The traction drops first near the surface, where the atoms arrive. It drops last at the interface, where the film is held: there no atoms leave the boundary and the boundary cannot open.
  3. At first, the deep traction rises. The material plated near the surface pushes the grains apart and loads the boundary below it. So the traction deeper down rises above σ0 before it decays; move the slider through the early times to see how far.
  4. At long times, an edge crack. The traction falls to zero along the whole boundary, and the plated wedge keeps the grains apart. The long-time wedge then has the opening of a traction-free edge crack of depth h in the same model: the two agree.

From the case study:

At first the deeper traction moves the other way. The material plated near the surface pushes the grains apart there and loads the part of the boundary below it, so the deeper traction rises before it decays: at the interface it is 1.010 σ₀ at t = 0.001 t₀ and 0.85 σ₀ at 0.05 t₀.

The paper finds the same end state from the stress intensity factor at the tip: it “rapidly approaches the value associated with a traction-free edge crack” (p. 2870). Steps 1 and 2 turn this picture into one equation. Steps 3 and 4 find how fast it relaxes, and step 5 what is left.

Step 1 Why atoms move, and the unit of time

Background: diffusion along a stressed grain boundary.

An atom on the flat free surface has chemical potential zero: it is the reference. On the boundary its chemical potential is μ = −σgbΩ (the paper’s Eq. 3), lower wherever the boundary is in tension. Atoms diffuse down the gradient of μ, so the flux along the boundary follows the gradient of the traction (Eq. 4). Where more atoms arrive than leave, they plate on the boundary and widen the wedge. This mass conservation makes the opening rate proportional to −∂2σgb/∂z2 (Eqs. 5 and 6):

The opening rate contains δgbDgbΩ/kT, and the elasticity of Eq. 2 contains E*. Making the equations dimensionless, with lengths in units of h, leaves a single unit of time: the paper’s t0 = 4πkTh3/(E*δgbDgbΩ) (Eq. 12). Every time in the solution is then a pure number times t0.

The equation is linear, so look for tractions that keep their shape and decay: separation of variables (Eq. 11). Each shape fn(x) is a traction mode, and λn is its decay rate. The conditions on σgb become conditions on each mode (Eqs. 13 and 15):

Step 4 The exact numbers and the relaxation time

Background: diffusion along a stressed grain boundary.

The exact decay rates need a numerical method that respects the singular structure of Eq. 13.

From the case study:

Integrating f‴ three times with the three conditions writes f as an integral of f‴ against a known kernel G(x, ξ), and turns the eigenproblem into a Cauchy-type singular integral equation for the dislocation density f‴. That density diverges as (1 − x)−1/2 at the interface: the wedge closes like a crack.

The paper solves this equation by collocation on Chebyshev points with the singularity built in (its Appendix A). Besides λ1 = 11.74265, the numerical solution gives the slowest mode’s traction at mid-depth: f1(0.5) = 0.8020, with f1(1) = 1 (the case’s reproduction of the paper’s collocation).

From the case study:

The slowest mode is zero at the free surface and largest at the interface: the boundary relaxes first where atoms arrive and last where the film is held. At mid-depth it is 0.802 of its interface value.

Step 5 What is left: an edge crack

Background: the stress intensity factor, mode I, the edge-crack factor.

From the case study:

Every mode decays, so σgb → 0 along the whole boundary while the plated wedge keeps it open. A cut from the surface to depth h with traction-free faces, in a half-space under σ₀, is an edge crack, and the stress intensity factor at the interface tends to 1.1215 σ₀√(πh).

1.1215 is the handbook factor for an edge crack of depth h in a half-space under uniform tension. The paper shows K approaching the edge-crack value in its Fig. 7, but does not print the number. The crack’s tip is at the interface, where the boundary meets the substrate.

From the case study:

The paper’s discussion draws the consequence: the wedge creates a crack-like stress concentration where the boundary meets the substrate without creating any free surface, and that concentration may nucleate dislocations in the film.

What happened

Agent B’s operator had one wrong sign, and only a comparison with something outside the operator could show it. Its residual and its stable digits were computed with the same wrong operator, so they passed. The crack-shape estimate and Agent A’s finite-difference schemes do not use that operator.

Agent A is Kimi K3, in the run MP-09. Agent B is DeepSeek, in the run MP-44, one of three reruns with its reasoning switched on.

The case study, Section 7, describes Agent B’s run as follows. The word for the reference answer is replaced by a description in square brackets.

MP-44 chose the right kind of basis, a weighted Chebyshev density with the Cauchy part done exactly. But its triple integral evaluated Tk(cos 2θ) where s = sin²θ requires Tk(2s − 1) = Tk(−cos 2θ), so every odd basis function had the wrong sign. It reported λ₁ = 88.25 and f₁(0.5) = 0.9705. Its four checks passed; none compared a basis function with its own third derivative, the link the sign broke. The grader flipped the odd columns and recovered [the reference answer]; our replay of its code with the one minus sign gives λ₁ = 11.74267, λ₂ = 264.635 and f₁(0.5) = 0.8020 at 40 unknowns.

Chebyshev polynomials of odd degree change sign with their argument, Tk(−y) = −Tk(y), and those of even degree do not. So the slip flipped every odd basis function and no even one.

Each answer was rerun from its reproduce command, checked automatically and graded blind by Codex on the three parts, with every error classified.

Codex graded Agent A right on all three parts, with no decisive error and twelve side errors, among them a wrong tip exponent in its text. It graded Agent B’s part (a) partly right, part (b) right and part (c), the time scale, wrong, with one decisive error. Of the round’s four automatic pass/fail checks, Agent B passed one: its K, the handbook value written into its code. The grades come from an AI model, and no expert has reviewed them.

6 Take away

The wedge tip is a crack tip; put it in the basis

The density diverges as (1 − x)−1/2 at the interface. The four exact Kimi and GLM runs built that into their basis. [One DeepSeek rerun] used up to 400 points of a plain Chebyshev basis for four digits, and [another] stalled about 1% off. The crack-shape estimate of step 3 has the same behaviour: its f1‴ = 3x/√(1 − x2) grows as (1 − x)−1/2 near x = 1.

A check that shares the defect cannot find it

[Agent B’s] residual and convergence used the same inconsistent basis; [a DeepSeek run with reasoning off]’s own-number checks passed with λ₁ twenty times too small. Compare with something the operator does not control. In Section 5, the crack-shape estimate of step 3 was such a comparison: it uses no operator at all.

A computed limit is a check; a remembered one is not

Computing K = 1.12152 from the kernel tests its sign and normalization. Four runs typed in 1.1215, and the K check passed them all, including two with λ₁ wrong. The working of step 5 shows the route that computes it: the traction-free equation of the same kernel.
