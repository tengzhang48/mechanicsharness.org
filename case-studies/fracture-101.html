Fracture Mechanics 101

A crack concentrates stress and releases stored energy when it grows. Material joining its faces can resist that growth. This page connects these physical pictures to the quantities used in MechanicsHarness cases. Read the explanation, use the formula, and check a small calculation before returning to your case.

How to use this page. Choose the term that stopped you. Check the conventions first if a length, sign or factor of two is unclear. The working is folded away so you can try each calculation yourself. All examples use hypothetical inputs, not measured material data.

0 Which length and which opening?

A centre crack has two ends, and both may advance. The original end is called the root; the advancing end is the current tip. Connectors are material connections that join the faces behind the tip. The opening is the distance between the faces, not the movement of one face.

In this growth picture, a is the original half-length, c is the current half-length, and L is the growth at each end.

In topics 6 and 7, a is the half-length of the open part and c is the half-length including the strips or bridged zones. These are the same letters as in the figure.

The coordinate x runs from the crack centre. The right root is at x = a, and the right tip is at x = c. The left side is their mirror image.

E′ is the effective elastic modulus. Here E is Young's modulus and ν is Poisson's ratio. Choose the branch matching the case's plane condition.

  • δ is the full face-to-face gap. For symmetric opening, it is twice either face's displacement from the symmetry plane.
  • Traction is force per unit area on a surface. Face pressure p > 0 opens the crack. A closing traction pulls the faces together and is represented by negative pressure.
  • G is the energy released, and Γ is the general symbol for the energy the material needs. Both are per unit crack-plane area. Count one crack area, not a separate area for each face.
  • Γ0 is the intrinsic toughness: the energy needed to separate material at the tip itself. Γb is the work absorbed by the connectors. Topics 5 and 7 explain the distinction.
  • Use Pa, m and J/m² in calculations. Stress intensity K, introduced next, is often reported in MPa √m. One MPa is 106 Pa. The symbol π is the circle constant.

Watch out. Growth of 5 mm at each end increases the total crack length by 10 mm, not 5 mm.

1 How strong is the stress concentration?

Pull a cracked plate and stress rises sharply near each tip. The stress intensity factor measures the strength of this near-tip stress field. At the same distance from the tip, a larger K means a larger stress. Opening the faces apart is called mode I.

The elastic model predicts a singularity: the stress grows without bound as the distance to the tip approaches zero. Real material instead yields or breaks in a small region called the separation zone.

KI is the mode-I stress intensity. Here Σ is the remote tensile stress, a is the crack half-length, and Y is a dimensionless geometry factor.

For a centre crack in a large plate, Y = 1. Other geometries need their own factor. Below, K means KI.

σyy is the tensile stress normal to the crack directly ahead of its tip. The distance from the tip is r.

Use this stress formula at distances larger than the separation zone but much smaller than the crack length. Such a region exists only if the separation zone is small compared with the crack.

Watch out. K is not a stress. Its units include a square root of length. The infinite elastic tip stress is not a physical material strength.

2 Is there enough energy to grow?

When a crack advances, the body becomes more compliant. Stored elastic energy can be released, and the loading system may supply work as the body deforms. The energy available for each increment of crack area is G.

G is the energy release rate, and E′ is the effective modulus from Conventions. The available energy scales with the square of the stress-field strength K and inversely with the stiffness E′.

Here “rate” means energy per increment of crack area, not per second. Energy toughness Γ has units J/m². K-toughness is the critical stress intensity for growth, usually reported in MPa √m. Substituting that critical intensity into the energy formula converts it to energy toughness.

Quasistatic growth is slow enough that inertia can be neglected. The crack stays put while G < Γ and can advance with G = Γ. Arrest means that the crack stops growing. If G > Γ, this criterion does not allow it to stop.

A quasistatic calculation follows successive states, not motion through time.

Watch out. Equality balances available and required energy. It does not say whether a small advance will settle or run away. That stability question is discussed in topic 7.

3 How do face loads change the tip field?

Linear elasticity lets us add solutions. A plate under remote tension with unloaded crack faces can be represented by an uncracked stretched plate plus a pressure-loaded crack. The pressure-loaded crack is an auxiliary problem: a simpler helper problem used to construct the real solution.

The auxiliary pressure cancels the forces that the uncracked solution carries across the future crack plane, leaving the combined faces unloaded. The uncracked solution has no singular stress field or face-to-face gap, so the auxiliary problem supplies all of K and the opening.

K is the right-tip intensity, c is the current half-length, x is the position from the centre, and p(x) is the symmetric face pressure.

This pressure solution is for a large plate with no remote load. The integral gives more weight to pressure near the tip. Thus, K depends on where the traction acts, not only on the total force.

Σ is the uniform opening pressure. The positive quantity σb is the magnitude of the closing traction exerted by connectors on a < |x| < c. The roots are at ±a, and the tips are at ±c.

To represent remote tension, use a uniform auxiliary face pressure equal to the remote tensile stress Σ. The second term subtracts the connectors' effect. This reduction of the tip intensity is called shielding. Evaluate arccos in radians.

Watch out. A positive connector strength represents a closing load, so its face pressure is negative. Adding its magnitude would increase the tip intensity.

4 What shape does an open crack take?

Uniform pressure pushes the upper face up and the lower face down. For a centre crack in a large elastic plate, the opening is elliptical. It is widest at the centre and zero at both tips.

δ(x) is the full gap at position x. Here p is the uniform positive face pressure, c is the crack half-length, and E′ is the effective modulus.

Use positions between the tips. Doubling the pressure doubles the opening; doubling the modulus halves it.

5 How much work do connectors absorb?

Connectors resist separation of the faces. A traction-gap law gives their resisting traction as the gap increases. The area under this curve is the work absorbed per unit crack-plane area, not a physical surface area. Constant traction gives a rectangular area up to the failure gap.

Γb is the connector work per unit crack-plane area. The function t(δ) gives the resisting traction magnitude at full gap δ. The failure gap is δc, and σb is the constant traction magnitude.

This is called a cohesive or bridging law: it describes how connections between the faces resist opening. Its work uses the positive resisting magnitude, although its equivalent face pressure is negative. Using the full gap already includes the work of both faces.

The wake is the region behind the advancing tip, where connectors absorb work. The tip only feels the stress field around it, whose intensity already includes the connectors' shielding; the connectors act behind it, so their work is accounted for separately.

The local tip growth condition therefore uses intrinsic toughness Γ0 alone. Do not put Γ0 + σbδc into that local condition. Topic 7 explains when their sum can represent the resistance of the whole crack.

Watch out. The area under the law is energy per unit area, not force. Do not multiply it by two for the two faces.

6 Can a crack open with zero tip intensity?

In Dugdale's strip-yield model, the strips represent a thin yielded zone where material has deformed permanently; here, they represent any zone that pulls the faces together with a constant traction. The central slit has no traction on its faces, and the resisting strips extend from its ends to the outer tips.

The remote stress and the closing tractions act together. At a particular load, their singular parts cancel at x = c, making K zero there. The opening is the net effect of both loads.

ΣD is the remote tensile stress giving zero outer-tip intensity. Here σb is the closing-traction magnitude, a is the half-length of the open part, and c is the half-length including the strips.

This load follows from topic 3's net-intensity formula by setting K = 0.

δ(a) is the full gap at the right root, and E′ is the effective modulus. The symbol ln means the natural logarithm.

The gap formula adds the openings produced by the remote stress and the closing tractions at this load. Zero K removes only the singular stress term at the outer tip; it does not remove the finite deformation produced throughout the body, so the central crack can remain open.

This constant-traction solution can describe either yielded strips or other closing connections. A tip that requires nonzero intrinsic toughness needs an additional tip field; this zero-K solution alone is not sufficient.

Watch out. The outer tip at x = c and the root at x = a are different places.

7 When can bridging become a toughness?

The bridged zone is the region behind the tip where connectors still join the faces. Small-scale bridging means that this zone is much shorter than the crack and the relevant body dimensions. In that limit, its total work can represent its contribution to toughness.

For a long zone, solve the elastic body equations and the connector law together. The body equations give the gap under the applied load and connector tractions. The law gives the traction at that gap, so both must agree.

ℓ is a material length scale. Here E′ is the effective modulus, δc is the connector failure gap, and σb is the constant resisting-traction magnitude.

ℓ is not the actual zone length. That length depends on loading, geometry and the traction law. The ratio Γ0 / (σbδc) also affects the solution; it does not by itself establish small-scale bridging.

Γ is the effective energy toughness, Γ0 is the intrinsic tip toughness, and Γb is the connector work per unit crack-plane area. This sum describes the resistance of the whole crack in the small-scale limit, not the local tip condition in topic 5.

This page cannot give a numerical threshold for “small-scale.” Compare the computed zone length, c − a at each end, with the crack length and the body dimensions, such as plate width and distances to nearby boundaries. The case studies do exactly that. For large-scale bridging, where the zone is not small by these comparisons, use both the body solution and the traction law.

Under load control, a small advance is stable if further growth requires a higher load. If further growth requires a lower load, the applied load can drive continued growth. The maximum marks the loss of this stability. Displacement control can behave differently, so identify what the loading system controls.

Watch out. A material length is not a computed zone size. An energy balance is not a stability guarantee.
